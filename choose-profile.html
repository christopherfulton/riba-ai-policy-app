Profile 1

Practices which may be smaller, and/or characterised by one or more of the following:

  • Requirement or desire for AI output to be more accurate or free of errors than is currently demonstrated.
  • Cautious risk appetite.
  • Prefer not to commit to increased digital operational cost investments, or have low expectations of return-on-investment of AI tools.
  • A chosen digital strategy to consciously avoid early-adoption of AI, preferring the value and stability represented by longer-established tools and choosing to “wait and see”, benefitting from others’ research and learning first.
  • Caution around professional, commercial or legal liability, or responding to advice to adopt a vigilant stance on AI by insurers/legal consultants
  • A client base or professional reputation likely to respond negatively to use of generative AI tools
  • Ethical or environmental/sustainability stance which conflicts with generative AI use
  • Concerns over training of AI models on copyrighted data
  • Conflicting cybersecurity or secure project requirements which curtail use or deployment of AI tools
  • Consider benefits more likely to come from established, deterministic processes and “traditional” automation (for example, computer programming or visual scripting) rather than non-deterministic LLM or agentic approaches.
  • Scepticism of the longevity or dependability of financial behaviour and economics around AI companies, or preferring not to become “locked-in” to a company or service that may not survive an economic “bubble” bursting.
  • Staff may be concerned that use of generative AI in test cases actually increases workload or time spent performing tasks, through necessary review and checking of large amounts of AI output.

Profile 2

"Yes, but..."

Practices that might be slightly larger in size, or characterised by one of more of these factors:

  • Willingness and interest in exploring/adopting AI tools along with a medium/measured risk appetite.
  • May already be budgeting for operational costs and testing of, for example, generative AI/LLM subscriptions provided by third-party technology companies.
  • Likely to be using off-the-shelf AI models or machine learning technologies, either “generalist” LLM models or architecture-specific AEC computational/iterative/generative tools.
  • Ready access to expertise (either internally or externally) – ability to oversee testing, analysis, securing, deploying and training relevant members in such technologies, in alignment with existing business policies.
  • Technical ability to research and trial potential AI tools, prior to deployment, in a safe, sandboxed or non-project environment.
  • IT and Data Security policies already in place and able to support permitted use of AI, and mature risk mitigation strategies.
  • Ability to critically and accurately evaluate and judge quantitative and qualitative impacts on efficiency/RoI, quality and productivity in architectural delivery, and react in an agile manner to such evaluations.
  • Obtained professional advice on legal risks and insurance cover, and have confidence in ability to engage with mitigation of commercial and legal risk factors.
  • Client base willing to support AI use, either as experimental research or in active project delivery.
  • Leadership and practice culture aligned in support of a measured and sensible approach to using AI.
  • Learning culture with strong engagement with AI-focussed CPD offerings.
  • Willingness to support a variety of AI adoption preferences amongst staff, from eager experimentation to cautious scepticism.
  • A process for restricting and approving services/tools, including applying to pilot/sandbox new tools.

Profile 3

"We're all in"

Practices with stated goals to engage in cutting-edge technology or identifying themselves as early-adopters or leaders in AI, perhaps characterised by one or more of:

  • Medium to large size.
  • High appetite for risk, able to accept both positive and negative potential impacts.
  • Practice financial foundations suitable to support operational investment required for compliant AI tool use, and any potential development of custom technological approaches.
  • Developed financial experience to be able to quantify return on investment.
  • Policy, IT, security and CPD/training infrastructure able to support a variety of AI types and tools, such as:
    • third-party “generalist” models
    • generative AI augmentation on company curated document/data libraries (for example, Retrieval Augmented Generation)
    • specific and bespoke/targeted machine learning applications applied to proprietary curated architectural datasets
    • full training, testing and/or extensive fine-tuning approaches to bespoke or self-hosted deep learning AI models (rarer, likely requiring significant infrastructure investment – for example, image models trained exclusively on internal IP to avoid copyright risks)
  • Likely to already have a bespoke AI policy embedded in practice culture and processes.
  • Designated lead/individual role or team for AI governance, approach and oversight, as a core part of business planning and board-level strategy.
  • AI evaluation, testing, deployment and use is supported by expert teams with relevant knowledge and skills within the business.
  • Strong training and development culture, able to provide members with necessary CPD and knowledge of both benefits and risks of AI use
  • Obtained professional advice on legal risks and insurance cover
  • An established process for restricting and approving tools, including applying to pilot/sandbox new tools.

Larger practices should have the internal governance capability to write their own bespoke AI policy directly, guided by RIBA's Core Principles of AI Use guidance document (link…), rather than working from a template.
